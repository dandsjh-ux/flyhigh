Dreaming of flight

For thousands of years, people watched birds and wondered what it would feel like to fly. Kites were flown in China more than 2,000 years ago, and inventors drew plans for flapping wings that never left the ground. The first real step came on November 21, 1783, when two passengers drifted over Paris, France, in a hot-air balloon built by the Montgolfier brothers. A balloon, however, simply floats wherever the wind pushes it. The true challenge was to build a flying machine that a pilot could steer.

The first powered flights

On December 17, 1903, at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville Wright lay on the lower wing of the Wright Flyer as it lifted off the sand. The flight lasted only 12 seconds and covered about 37 metres, which is shorter than the wingspan of a modern jumbo jet. Still, it was the first time a powered machine that was heavier than air had carried a person in controlled flight. The Wright brothers succeeded because they solved three problems at once: they built wings that made enough lift, an engine light enough to provide thrust, and controls that let the pilot balance and turn.

Canada was not far behind. On February 23, 1909, J.A.D. McCurdy flew the Silver Dart from the frozen surface of Bras d'Or Lake at Baddeck, Nova Scotia. The plane had been designed by the Aerial Experiment Association, a group led by Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor famous for the telephone. McCurdy flew about 800 metres, and the Silver Dart made the first powered, controlled flight in Canada.

How does a plane fly?

Four forces act on every airplane in flight. Lift pushes the plane up. It is made by air flowing over and under the wings. Weight (gravity) pulls the plane down. Thrust, from a propeller or a jet engine, pushes the plane forward. Drag is the push of the air against the plane, and it slows the plane down. To climb, lift must be greater than weight. To speed up, thrust must be greater than drag.

Wings go to war

During the First World War (1914–1918), airplanes changed quickly. At first they were used only to spy on enemy troops, but soon pilots were fighting in the sky. Most of these early planes were biplanes, with two sets of wings stacked one above the other and held together by wooden struts and wires. Their frames were made of wood and covered with fabric, so they were light but fragile. By the Second World War (1939–1945), most new fighters and bombers were monoplanes, with a single set of wings, and they were built from aluminum, a metal that is light and strong.

One Canadian engineer played a key role in that second war. Elsie MacGill was the first woman in Canada to earn a degree in electrical engineering, and she went on to study aircraft design. During the war, she was the chief aeronautical engineer at a factory in Fort William, Ontario (now part of Thunder Bay), that built more than 1,400 Hawker Hurricane fighter planes. Her team adapted the Hurricane for Canadian winters by adding skis and de-icing equipment. A comic book about her life called her the "Queen of the Hurricanes."

Bush planes open the North

In the 1920s and 1930s, Canada's North had very few roads or railways. Bush pilots changed that. They flew small planes into remote communities, carrying mail, food, medicine, mining equipment and people. In winter, their planes landed on skis on frozen lakes. In summer, they landed on floats on open water.

One famous flight took place in January 1929. A disease called diphtheria had broken out near Fort Vermilion, in northern Alberta, and the people there needed medicine quickly. Pilots Wilfrid "Wop" May and Vic Horner flew north from Edmonton in a small plane with an open cockpit, in bitter cold of about –30 °C. To keep the medicine from freezing, they wrapped it in wool blankets with a small charcoal heater. They delivered it safely, and large crowds cheered when they returned to Edmonton.

After the Second World War, the de Havilland Canada company in Toronto designed a plane made just for the bush. The DHC-2 Beaver first flew in 1947. It could take off from short, rough strips, and it could be fitted with wheels, floats or skis. More than 1,600 Beavers were built, and many are still flying today. In 1987, the Beaver was named one of the top ten Canadian engineering achievements of the twentieth century.

The jet age

Propellers have a speed limit. As the tips of the blades spin close to the speed of sound, they lose efficiency, and the plane cannot go much faster. The jet engine solved this problem. Instead of spinning a propeller, a jet engine sucks in air, squeezes it, mixes it with fuel and burns it, then blasts hot gas out the back to create thrust. The first jet-powered plane flew in Germany in 1939. Ten years later, on August 10, 1949, the Avro Canada C102 Jetliner took off near Toronto. It was the second jet airliner in the world to fly, only two weeks after Britain's de Havilland Comet.

Canada's most famous jet is one that never entered service. The Avro Arrow was a supersonic interceptor, designed to fly at about twice the speed of sound. It first flew on March 25, 1958, and many people believed it was the most advanced fighter of its time. Then, on February 20, 1959, the government cancelled the program because it cost so much. Thousands of workers lost their jobs, and the finished planes were cut up for scrap. People still argue about whether the decision was right.

Meanwhile, jet airliners grew bigger and faster. The Boeing 747 "jumbo jet" first flew in 1969 and could carry more than 350 passengers. Concorde, a supersonic airliner built by Britain and France, made the transatlantic trip from London to New York in about three and a half hours. It carried passengers from 1976 until 2003. Air travel slowly became something that ordinary families could afford.

Types of planes today

Today's aircraft come in many shapes and sizes, and each type is designed for a different job. The table below compares some of the most common types. Notice how the job a plane must do decides what kind of engine and wings it needs.

Water bombers deserve special mention, because Canada builds some of the best in the world. The Canadair CL-215 first flew in 1967. Its newer version, the CL-415, can skim across a lake and scoop up about 6,000 litres of water in roughly 12 seconds without stopping. It then climbs away and drops the water on a forest fire. Countries around the world, from France to Greece, fight fires with Canadian-designed water bombers.

The future of flight

Airplanes burn a lot of fuel, and burning fuel releases carbon dioxide, a gas that contributes to climate change. Engineers are now searching for cleaner ways to fly. On December 10, 2019, Harbour Air, a seaplane company in British Columbia, flew a Beaver that had been fitted with an electric motor. It was the first flight of an all-electric commercial airplane. For now, electric planes can only fly short distances because batteries are heavy. Some experts believe that small electric aircraft could one day carry passengers on short trips between cities and islands.

In just over a hundred years, airplanes went from a 12-second hop on a sandy beach to jets that circle the globe. The next big change in flight might be quieter, cleaner and closer to home than anyone expects.

Glossary

biplane
an airplane with two sets of wings, one above the other
cancel
to stop a plan or project before it is finished (past tense: cancelled)
drag
the push of the air against a moving plane, which slows it down
lift
the upward force made by air moving over and under the wings
monoplane
an airplane with one set of wings
remote
far away from towns and cities, and hard to reach
supersonic
faster than the speed of sound
thermal
a rising column of warm air
thrust
the force that pushes an aircraft forward
turbine
a machine with blades that spin when gas or liquid flows through it
